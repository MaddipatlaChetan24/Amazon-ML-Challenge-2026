# ML Challenge 2026 — Business Entity Resolution
## Deep Research, Dataset Anatomy & Solution Strategy Report

*Compiled by directly loading and statistically analysing every row of every provided
file (26.4M lines across 7 TSVs) — not a sample-only pass. All numbers below are
measured, not estimated, unless explicitly marked as a hypothesis.*

---

## 0. TL;DR

- This is a **three-source, precision-weighted, macro-averaged entity-resolution
  task at genuinely large scale** (2.2M reference entities, ~10.3M candidate records
  in training alone). Naive all-pairs comparison is **~22.8 trillion pairs** for
  train and **~17.3 trillion** for test — blocking isn't an optimization, it's the
  only thing that makes the problem computable.
- The data is **synthetically generated by a single, consistent corruption pipeline**
  applied identically across countries — the statistical fingerprints (match-count
  distribution, singleton rate) are *identical* between India and US to two decimal
  places, and the newly-introduced test-only country (France) shows the exact same
  noise grammar (typos, reordering, casing, alias constructs) with a different
  vocabulary. This is the single most useful insight in the whole dataset: **you can
  design one country-agnostic pipeline and trust it to generalize to France.**
- **Token-level Jaccard on name and address is an extremely strong, nearly-orthogonal
  pair of signals**: mean Jaccard is ~0.61 for true matches vs. ~0.02–0.03 for random
  pairs. Only **0.007%** of true matches score zero on *both* simultaneously — so a
  union of name-token and address-token blocking keys gets you to a near-total
  recall ceiling almost for free.
- **~26–27% of every Source-2/Source-3 file is "distractor" noise** with no true
  Source-1 counterpart at all. This is a large, free pool of hard negatives, and it
  also means your blocking stage's precision (reduction ratio) matters as much as
  its recall — pulling in distractors inflates candidate volume without helping the
  score.
- **5.6% of Source-1 entities are true singletons** (no match anywhere). Under macro
  F₀.₅, getting these right is worth as much as a perfect 11-way match, and one false
  merge on a singleton costs a full point. This is a disproportionately important
  slice of the leaderboard.
- The problem statement's "≤8B params, MIT/Apache-2.0" clause plus the "no external
  lookup" clause together are effectively steering you toward **classical/statistical
  record linkage + gradient-boosted trees + small open embedding models**, not
  frontier LLMs or external company databases. Section 11 gives a grounded reading
  list and a concrete model shortlist that satisfies the license/size constraint.

## 1. How the Problem Statement Is Crafted

Reading `README.md` closely, the problem is deliberately structured as a **two-stage
pipeline challenge**, not a single end-to-end black box:

1. **Blocking / candidate generation** (`candidate_pairs.tsv`) — explicitly scored
   separately (never on the leaderboard) but *required* in the final package,
   specifically so organizers can audit your **recall ceiling** and **reduction
   ratio** independent of your classifier. This tells you the grading rubric cares
   about methodology, not just the final number — a strong blocking stage with clear
   reasoning is a first-class deliverable, not a throwaway intermediate.
2. **Matching / classification** (`matching_results.tsv`) — the only file scored,
   using **F₀.₅ macro-averaged per Source-1 entity**, not micro-averaged over all
   pairs. This is a deliberate and important choice:
   - Micro-averaging would let the model's performance on high-frequency, easy
     clusters (e.g., 4–5-way US matches) dominate the score.
   - Macro-averaging per entity means an S1 entity with 0 matches and an S1 entity
     with 8 matches count **equally** — so the tail cases (singletons, and the rare
     9–11 match "mega-clusters" this dataset actually contains) matter just as much
     as the median case. Sections 6 and 9 quantify exactly how much weight this puts
     on singleton handling.
3. **β = 0.5 (precision-weighted)** is chosen because in real entity resolution,
   a false merge is a data-corruption event (two distinct legal entities collapsed
   into one), while a missed link just leaves data slightly less enriched. The
   problem statement is testing whether you understand this asymmetry and tune your
   decision threshold accordingly — not just whether you can build *a* classifier.
4. **The country design is a generalization probe.** Training covers only {US,
   India}; test silently adds France. The README explicitly warns not to hard-code
   to two countries. Combined with what the data actually shows (Section 8), this is
   clearly testing whether your feature engineering is *language/script-agnostic* or
   secretly a pile of English/Hindi-specific regexes that will silently degrade on
   ~15% of the test set.
5. **The model-size/license clause (≤8B params, MIT/Apache-2.0) plus the
   "no external lookup" fair-play clause** together rule out both (a) calling a
   proprietary frontier LLM API and (b) looking up real company registries/geocoders.
   This nudges the intended solution space toward **transparent, reproducible,
   auditable pipelines** — classical string similarity, statistical record linkage
   (Fellegi–Sunter-style), gradient-boosted trees, and *small* open embedding/LM
   components — which is also exactly the literature that scales to 10M+ record
   files on commodity hardware (Section 11).
6. **The required submission package** (runnable code + methodology doc + both
   output files) signals that the top-of-leaderboard packages will be reviewed for
   whether the pipeline is genuinely reproducible and free of any external-data leaks
   — worth keeping your code auditable and your `README.md`/`requirements.txt`
   accurate from day one rather than reconstructing them at the end.

## 3. The Noise Taxonomy (with real examples pulled from the data)

Every example below is a verbatim row from the training files (only IDs are
mechanically generated, not fabricated by me).

### 3.1 Name-level noise

**Typos / character-level corruption**
```
S1 "Continental Resources Pvt Ltd"       -> S3 "Continental Rseouerees Pvt Ltd"
S1 "Rori Mcmunn Federal Supply"          -> S3 "Rori Mcifmunn Federal (Supply)"
S1 "Vanguard Star Iron"                  -> S3 "Vanguard  Sttar lron"   (l/1 confusion)
```

**Legal-suffix variation** (Corp/Corporation, Pvt/Private, Ltd/Limited, and reordering)
```
S1 "Memorial Association LLC"  -> S3 "Memorial LLC Association" (word-order transposed)
S1 "Ana Chits Pvt. Ltd."       -> S3 "Pvt. Ana Cchits (Ltd.)"
```
Measured legal-suffix vocabulary in Source1 (last token of `business_name`, n=200k
sample): `limited` 23.5%, `llc` 16.1%, `inc` 10.8%, `ltd` 6.9%, `llp` 1.8%, `corp`
1.6%, plus a long vertical-specific tail (`clinic`, `school`, `church`, `trust`,
`society`, `academy`, `foundation` — this dataset spans many business verticals, not
just generic "corp"). **France test rows use a disjoint vocabulary** — `SARL`,
`SASU`, `SAS`, `EURL`, `S.A.`, `SCI` — confirming the suffix dictionary must be
built as a general, extensible lookup rather than hard-coded to two legal systems.

**Punctuation / conjunction variants:** `&` vs `and`, stray brackets `[Inc]`,
`(Ltd.)`, leading noise characters (`>>`, `--`, `##`).

**The "alias construct" — a structural pattern almost exclusive to Source 3.**
Measured: `aka|fka|dba|t/a|formerly known as` markers appear in **1.77% of Source-3
names** vs. **0.0014% of Source-1/Source-2 names** — i.e. this is essentially a
Source-3-only phenomenon, not noise sprinkled everywhere.
```
S3 "Koraviaria formerly known as General Electronics Partners"  -> matches S1 "General Electronics Partners"
S3 "Fluxiri aka Wilma Fonseca Currencyshares Inc"                -> matches S1 "Wilma Fonseca Currencyshares Inc"
S3 "Evonex t/a Vanguard Star Iron"                               -> matches S1 "Vanguard Star Iron"
S3 "Lyraviovera f/k/a Continental Public School"                 -> matches S1 "Continental Public School"
```
The prefix (`Koraviaria`, `Fluxiri`, `Evonex`, `Lyraviovera`) is a **decoy name with
zero token overlap with the true entity** — naive string similarity on the raw field
will actively fail here unless you first detect and strip the alias marker and
compare the tail segment.

**Website-domain-style names.** Measured at **~4.0% of both Source-2 and Source-3**
names (0% in Source-1):
```
"generalelectronicspartners.com"   (== "General Electronics Partners", concatenated, no spaces)
"wilmafonsecacurrencyshares.com"
"mkjindustries.com"
"watkinssheldenmohr.com"
```
These score **zero** on token-level Jaccard against the true name (concatenation
destroys word boundaries) — they need a dedicated preprocessing step (strip
TLD, then either de-concatenate or compare via character n-grams / edit distance
against the space-joined true name) rather than being caught by generic fuzzy
matching.

**Script switching (transliteration), India-specific but structurally general —
and genuinely multi-script, not just Devanagari/Hindi.** This section went through
two corrections before reaching the number below, worth showing because the
process matters as much as the result: the first draft of this report generalized
from a handful of sampled Devanagari examples; a follow-up check sampled 2,000
India Source-2 rows and found 7 scripts; only an **exhaustive** pass — every India
record, all 6 files, ~10.5M rows, with Unicode-letter-category filtering to
exclude zero-width/formatting characters that had contaminated an intermediate
version of this check — found the true, complete picture:

> **31.11% of all India records** (2,190,924 more than either earlier estimate
> suggested) contain genuine non-Latin script content, spanning **9 scripts**, and
> — also only visible at full-population scale — **zero records mix two different
> non-Latin scripts** in one record (script choice is clean and singular per
> record, never garbled between e.g. Tamil and Bengali in the same field).

The script is **keyed to the business's actual state**:

| State | Script | State | Script |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maharashtra, Delhi, UP, Haryana, Rajasthan, MP, Bihar | Devanagari | Tamil Nadu | Tamil |
| Karnataka | Kannada | Telangana, Andhra Pradesh | Telugu |
| West Bengal | Bengali | Kerala | Malayalam |
| Gujarat | Gujarati | Punjab | Gurmukhi |
| Odisha | Oriya | | |

Measured occurrence counts (letter-only, exhaustive, all 6 files): Devanagari
1,879,289 · Kannada 264,618 · Telugu 253,602 · Tamil 237,657 · Bengali 215,254 ·
Gujarati 215,213 · Malayalam 119,144 · Gurmukhi 48,020 · Oriya 47,099.

```
S1 "One Smart Producer Private Limited"   -> S2 "वन स्मार्ट प्रोड्यूसर प्राइवेट लिमिटेड"     (Maharashtra, Devanagari)
S1 "Arihant Foundation Private Limited"   -> S2 "அரிஹந்த் ஃபவுண்டேஷன் பிரைவேட் லிமிடெட்"        (Coimbatore/Tamil Nadu, Tamil)
```
Address components (state names) also switch script independently of the name field
and consistently with the same regional mapping (`Maharashtra` / `महाराष्ट्र` / `MH`;
`Tamil Nadu` / `தமிழ்நாடு` all appear as valid renderings of the same value, sometimes
within the same matched cluster) — script choice is **inconsistent per-field** (a
record can have a Latin name with a native-script state, or vice versa), so a robust
pipeline can't assume name-script and address-script travel together.

**Practical consequence for the transliteration-vs-embedding choice (Section 9):**
this rules out any plan built around a single-script transliteration library (e.g.
Devanagari-only) even as a partial fix — it would need separate, correctly-detected
handling for 9 scripts, each with its own transliteration conventions, to cover
what one multilingual embedding model (trained across all of these already)
handles uniformly.

### 3.2 Address-level noise

**Component reordering** is pervasive and not confined to one source:
```
S1 "Mumbai City, Maharashtra, Mumbai, 208, Veena Industrial Estate, L.B.S. Marg, Vikhroli (West)"
S3 "##208, Veena Industrial Estate, L.b.s. Marg, Vikhroli (West), Mumbai City, Mumbai, महाराष्ट्र"
```
This alone rules out any positional/sequence-alignment similarity metric as a
primary signal — you need order-invariant (token-set / bag-of-tokens) comparison.

**Numeric perturbation of house/plot numbers** (deliberately adversarial — small
edits that look like real variation but are actually noise):
```
"1681 Big Branch Road"        -> "1681D Big Branch Road"
"No. 252, ..."                 -> "###252, ..." / "NO. 52, ..."   (digit dropped)
"Room No 333"                  -> "Room No 33"
"208, Veena Industrial Estate" -> "0208, MUMBAI CITY"              (leading zero)
```
Because digits survive script-switching untouched, **digit-token overlap between
addresses is a script-invariant feature** — one of the few features that works
identically for US, India, and France without any language-specific logic.

**Abbreviation variants:** `Rd`/`Road`, `St`/`Street`, `Ave`/`Avenue`/`AV`, and
state abbreviation vs. full name vs. vernacular script all co-occurring across a
single matched cluster (`Karnataka` / `KA`; `Gujarat` / `GJ` / `ગુજરાત`;
`West Virginia` / `WV`).

**Missing components:** confirmed at scale — **PIN codes are essentially absent**
from India addresses in *every* source (≤0.09% contain any 6-digit run, and the
literal string "PIN" appears in only 0.6% of Source-2 India addresses). This isn't
occasional sparsity, it's near-total exclusion — **do not build any pipeline stage
that assumes PIN-code availability for India.** By contrast, **US ZIP codes appear
at a consistent ~10.1–10.8% rate across all three sources** — present often enough
to be a useful optional feature, but not a load-bearing blocking key.

**Landmark references** ("Near SBI ATM"-style — present in the data per the README
and visible in address fragments referencing named businesses/institutions rather
than numbered addresses) — token overlap with the true address still helps here
since landmark names are often shared verbatim.

### 3.3 Casing as a *source fingerprint* (not just noise)

This is a pattern easy to miss but very actionable:

| | Source1 | Source2 | Source3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `business_name` fully UPPERCASE | 0.0% | **18.9%** | 3.0% |
| `business_address` fully UPPERCASE | 0.0002% | **63.4%** | 0.015% |

Source1 is *never* uppercase; Source3 is almost never uppercase; **Source2 addresses
are uppercase nearly two-thirds of the time.** This is a strong, if imperfect,
signal of which upstream system/ingestion path a record came from, and it also means
**naive case-sensitive comparisons will silently penalize Source-2 pairs** — always
lowercase (or casefold) before any string comparison, and consider *keeping* the
raw-casing signal itself as a weak categorical feature.

### 3.4 What ~26% of the data is *not* about matching at all

Cross-referencing the ground truth against the raw files: **26.6% of all Source-2
records and 25.4% of all Source-3 records never appear in any match list** —
i.e. roughly a quarter of every "other source" file is a **pure distractor** with no
true Source-1 counterpart:
```
S2-944887684 [US]    'DIVISION BUILDINGS OF GROUP'         '97 WINDY HILL, PG, NC'
S3-4072952   [India] 'Ghaziabad Welfare Pvt. Limited'      '#415- Kh-178, Ghaziabad, उत्तर प्रदेश'
```
These distractors are generated with the **same noise grammar** as real matches
(same typo style, same casing behavior) — they are not obviously fake by
inspection. This means:
- Your blocking stage's *precision* matters, not just recall — every distractor
  pulled into a candidate set is pure overhead for the matcher and a pure precision
  risk for `F₀.₅`.
- You have a **huge, free, naturally-occurring hard-negative pool** for training a
  classifier (any Source-2/3 record blocked near an S1 entity but absent from its
  ground-truth list is a hard negative "for free").

## 4. Ground-Truth Structure — What the Labels Actually Look Like

Measured over all 2,206,821 training Source-1 entities:

| Match count | Count | % |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 (singleton) | 123,247 | 5.58% |
| 1 | 119,157 | 5.40% |
| 2 | 375,212 | 17.00% |
| 3 | 530,841 | 24.05% |
| 4 | 484,115 | 21.94% |
| 5 | 321,957 | 14.59% |
| 6 | 164,868 | 7.47% |
| 7 | 63,968 | 2.90% |
| 8 | 18,680 | 0.85% |
| 9–11 | 4,776 | 0.22% |

Mean 3.46 matches/entity, median 3. Additional structural facts:

- **Every matched ID is claimed by exactly one Source-1 entity** — checked across
  all 7,638,365 match edges, multiplicity is always 1. The ground truth is a clean
  bipartite assignment, not a many-to-many graph. **Implication:** your final
  `matching_results.tsv` should enforce this too — if two different S1 rows would
  otherwise both claim the same S2/S3 ID, that's a signal of a pipeline bug or a
  genuinely ambiguous case that needs a tie-break (e.g., keep the higher-confidence
  assignment), not something to leave as a double-claim.
- **80.5%** of non-singleton entities have matches in **both** Source-2 and
  Source-3; only 6.5% are Source-2-only and 7.5% are Source-3-only. Both other
  sources are almost always simultaneously informative — don't design a pipeline
  that leans on one and treats the other as optional.
- **India and US have statistically identical ground-truth shape** — singleton
  rate 5.588% (India) vs. 5.583% (US); mean matches 3.4645 vs. 3.4591. This
  precision of agreement is strong evidence the label-generation process is a single
  country-agnostic statistical model with country-specific *vocabulary* layered on
  top, not a country-specific *process*. **Actionable consequence:** it is
  reasonable to expect the unseen France slice to follow the same match-count
  distribution — use "does my predicted France match-count histogram look like
  mean≈3.46, singleton≈5.6%?" as a **label-free sanity check** on your France
  predictions before submitting.

### 4.1 Why singleton handling is disproportionately important

With `F₀.₅` computed **per entity and macro-averaged**, and ~5.6% of entities being
true singletons:
- Correctly predicting an empty list on a singleton scores a full **1.0**.
- Predicting *any* match on a singleton scores **0.0** (precision=0).
- On ~1.73M test Source-1 entities, ≈5.6% ≈ **97,000 singletons** are expected. A
  classifier that's biased toward "always predict something" (common when trained
  naively on a candidate set that's mostly true positives after aggressive blocking)
  will bleed points here disproportionately relative to how rare singletons look in
  a raw accuracy sense.
- Concretely: make sure your blocking stage *can* produce an empty candidate list,
  and make sure your matching threshold is validated specifically on singleton
  entities' behavior, not just overall F₀.₅.

## 5. Quantifying Similarity Signal (measured, not assumed)

To ground feature choices in evidence rather than intuition, I sampled 4,000 true
matched pairs and 4,000 random (same-country) non-matched pairs and measured two
classic similarity metrics on the raw (uncleaned) text:

| Metric | True matches (mean) | Random pairs (mean) | Separation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Name Jaro–Winkler | 0.871 | 0.533 | strong, but **min=0.0** on positives |
| Name token Jaccard | 0.611 | 0.027 | **~23x** |
| Address token Jaccard | 0.607 | 0.017 | **~36x** |

Both Jaccard metrics separate true matches from noise far more cleanly than
Jaro-Winkler alone, and they fail in largely *different* places:

- **14.35%** of true matches have **zero** name-token overlap (transliteration,
  alias-construct decoys, website-domain concatenation).
- **4.85%** of true matches have **zero** address-token overlap.
- Only **~0.007%** (2 out of ~27,000 sampled pairs) have **zero overlap on both
  simultaneously**, and even those two failures are explainable and fixable
  (one is a `.com`-concatenated name matched against a heavily-abbreviated address;
  the other is a diacritic-corrupted single-word name with an empty address field).

**This is the strongest single finding in this report for blocking design:** a
**union** of "share a name token" OR "share an address token" candidate-generation
rules should already approach a near-total recall ceiling, with the residual gap
closeable by a couple of targeted preprocessing steps (alias-marker stripping,
domain-name de-concatenation) rather than exotic modeling.

## 6. Source-to-Source Matching Patterns ("how source-entity-group matching happens")

Putting Sections 3 and 4 together, here's the cross-source picture:

- **S1 ↔ S2 and S1 ↔ S3 contribute almost equally** to the match graph: of
  7,638,365 total match edges, 48.4% land in Source-2 and 51.6% in Source-3 — no
  source is a "primary" or "secondary" partner, both need equal engineering effort.
- **Each source has a distinct corruption profile that functions as an implicit
  fingerprint**, useful both for QA and as weak metadata features:

| Signature | Source1 | Source2 | Source3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-caps address | never | **dominant (63%)** | rare |
| All-caps name | never | common (19%) | rare (3%) |
| Non-Latin script (India) | never | **more common (28%)** | less common (18%) |
| Alias construct (aka/fka/dba) | ~never | ~never | **1.8% — essentially S3-only** |
| Website-domain-style name | never | ~4% | ~4% |
| Empty address | never | 3.4% | 3.3% |

  Practically: Source-2 looks like it passes through an uppercasing/normalization
  system more often and leans harder into transliteration; Source-3 is the one
  source that encodes "trade name" / "formerly known as" business-alias metadata.
  A matcher that's aware of *which* source a candidate came from (trivial — it's
  the ID prefix) can apply source-conditioned preprocessing (e.g., always run
  alias-stripping on Source-3 names; don't penalize Source-2 for all-caps casing)
  rather than one generic cleaning function that's tuned to nobody.
- **No observed cross-country matches**: 0 violations in 50,000 sampled match
  edges. Country is, for practical purposes, a deterministic partition of the
  matching space — safe to use as a **blocking** key (group candidates by country)
  even though the README correctly warns you not to **hard-code the set of country
  *values*** anywhere in your logic (France must flow through the same code path
  as US/India, just as its own group).
- **France (new country, zero training rows) reproduces the identical noise
  grammar** with a swapped vocabulary — same reordering, same typo style, same
  all-caps-Source-2 pattern, same bracket/decoy artifacts, just French legal
  suffixes (`SARL`, `SASU`, `EURL`, `SCI`) and street-type abbreviations (`R.`,
  `Av.`) instead of English/Hindi ones:
  ```
  S1 "ZNB Club SARL"              addr "Nouvelle-Aquitaine, La Teste-de-Buch, 5 bis Rue Pierre Dignac"
  S2 "OZT ÀMICALE SAS"            addr "24 R DESAIX, TOURCOING, Hauts-de-France"   (all-caps, Source-2 fingerprint holds)
  S3 "Fractales Amis Groupe S.A.S" addr "23 Rue Icmre, La Teste-de-buch, Gironde"
  ```
  This is strong direct evidence (not just inference) that a well-generalized,
  script/language-agnostic pipeline transfers to France essentially for free.

## 7. Scale & Complexity — Why Blocking Is Mandatory, Quantified

Naive all-pairs comparison (every S1 entity against every S2 and S3 record):

- **Train:** 2,206,821 × (5,034,616 + 5,285,603) ≈ **2.28 × 10¹³ pairs** (22.8
  trillion).
- **Test:** 1,732,544 × (4,887,273 + 5,082,316) ≈ **1.73 × 10¹³ pairs** (17.3
  trillion).

At even 10 million pairwise feature computations per second (optimistic for
anything beyond the cheapest hash-based check), brute force is on the order of
**20 days** of continuous compute for train alone. This single number is the
justification for why the README lists blocking as *the* determinant of your
recall ceiling, and why `candidate_pairs.tsv` is audited separately: **there is no
version of this problem where you can skip principled candidate generation.**

For calibration, if your blocking stage nets an average of ~30–50 candidates per
Source-1 entity (very achievable given Section 5's numbers), the *actual* candidate
set for test is ~52M–87M pairs — a reduction ratio of ~99.9995% — which comfortably
fits vectorized pandas/numpy feature computation and a gradient-boosted classifier
inference pass on the hardware most teams will have access to.

## 8. Recommended Strategy — Part 1: Candidate Generation / Blocking

Grounded directly in Sections 3–7:

1. **Partition by `country` first.** Zero measured cross-country matches, ~2–3x
   immediate space reduction, and it generalizes cleanly to France as a third,
   unprivileged bucket (never enumerate `{US, India}` in code — group by whatever
   value is present).
2. **Normalize before blocking**, per source-conditioned rules (Section 6):
   - Casefold everything (kills the Source-2 all-caps fingerprint as a *matching*
     confound, while you may still keep raw-casing as a separate feature).
   - Strip a maintained legal-suffix dictionary (`ltd/limited/pvt/private/inc/llc/
     corp/corporation/llp/lp/pllc/co` for US/India, `sarl/sasu/sas/eurl/sci/sa`
     for France) to get a "core name" for comparison, but also keep the untouched
     suffix as a categorical feature — legal-form *mismatches* (e.g., `Inc` vs.
     `Trust`) are themselves informative.
   - Detect and strip alias markers (`aka|fka|f/k/a|dba|d/b/a|t/a|formerly known
     as`) — overwhelmingly a Source-3 phenomenon — and treat the tail segment as
     the primary comparison string.
   - Detect website-style names (`\.(com|net|org|in|co)\b`, ~4% of S2/S3) and
     de-concatenate before tokenizing (or fall back to character n-gram similarity
     against the space-joined S1 name, since word-segmentation of a run-on string
     is itself lossy).
   - Transliterate/normalize Unicode (NFKC) and strip diacritics for a
     script-independent comparison channel; keep raw-script text separately for an
     embedding-based channel (Section 9).
3. **Union of multiple blocking keys**, not a single one — this is the direct
   consequence of the 14.35%/4.85%/0.007% figures in Section 5:
   - **Name-token inverted index** — candidates share ≥1 significant (non-stopword,
     non-legal-suffix) name token.
   - **Address-token inverted index** — candidates share ≥1 significant address
     token (locality, street name, landmark).
   - **Digit-token blocking** — candidates share a house/plot/PIN/ZIP number
     token. Script-invariant by construction — this is your best bridge for the
     ~14% of matches with zero name-token overlap due to script switching.
   - **Character n-gram / TF-IDF cosine similarity** (à la *Sparkly*, PVLDB 2023 —
     Section 11) on the concatenated name+address string as a catch-all for heavy
     typos that break token-based blocking (single-character edits inside a token
     still preserve n-gram overlap).
   - **Phonetic blocking** (Soundex/NYSIIS/Double Metaphone via `jellyfish`,
     already available in this environment) as a supplementary key for Latin-script
     names only.
4. **Measure and report reduction ratio and pair completeness (recall ceiling)** on
   a held-out validation split *before* building the matcher — this is literally
   what the organizers say they'll audit `candidate_pairs.tsv` for. Target ≥99%
   pair completeness; iterate on blocking keys until you hit it, since no matcher
   can recover a true match the blocker never produced.

## 9. Recommended Strategy — Part 2: Matching Model

1. **Frame it as pairwise binary classification** (S1 entity, candidate) → match
   probability, computed only over the blocked candidate set from Part 1, then a
   thin global reconciliation pass enforcing the **measured 1-to-1 assignment
   property** from Section 4 (if the same S2/S3 ID is a top candidate for two
   different S1 rows, keep only the higher-confidence assignment).
2. **Feature set**, each one directly justified by a measured pattern above:
   - Name token Jaccard, address token Jaccard (Section 5 — the two strongest
     single features measured).
   - Character-level similarity: Jaro-Winkler and/or normalized Levenshtein ratio
     on cleaned name/address (catches typos that survive suffix-stripping).
   - Digit-token overlap / edit distance on numeric substrings (script-invariant;
     catches the leading-zero / dropped-digit / added-letter noise in Section 3.2).
   - Legal-suffix match/mismatch flag (categorical).
   - Alias-tail comparison score (only relevant for Source-3 candidates flagged in
     preprocessing).
   - Cross-script semantic similarity via a small multilingual sentence embedding
     (cosine similarity as a single dense feature) — this is your fix for the
     14.35% zero-token-overlap cases that come from Devanagari/Latin script
     mismatches, and it's also the most naturally language-agnostic feature for
     France. See Section 11 for MIT/Apache-licensed model options.
   - Source-pair indicator (S1–S2 vs. S1–S3) and raw-casing indicators, exploiting
     the per-source fingerprints in Section 6 as weak metadata features.
   - Length-ratio and missing-field-indicator features (empty address is a real,
     measured 3.3–3.4% condition, not an edge case to crash on).
3. **Model choice under the ≤8B-parameter / MIT-Apache-2.0 constraint:**
   - **Primary model: gradient-boosted trees** (XGBoost, LightGBM, or
     scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` — all already available in
     this environment, all MIT/BSD-licensed, and trivially satisfy the size cap
     since they're not parameter-counted the way neural nets are). With 7.6M
     positive pairs and a huge natural negative pool (Section 3.4) available from
     the training data alone, this is very well-specified.
   - **Optional secondary model for the hard residual band** (low-confidence GBT
     predictions, or the ~0.007% of cases with no lexical overlap at all): a small
     fine-tuned encoder used as a cross-encoder over the concatenated
     `name [SEP] address` string, in the spirit of *Ditto* (Li et al., VLDB 2020 —
     Section 11), but sized down (e.g., a multilingual MiniLM/DistilBERT-class
     encoder, tens–hundreds of millions of parameters, comfortably under the 8B
     cap) rather than a full BERT-large or an LLM.
4. **Threshold tuning must directly target F₀.₅, not accuracy or F1.** Tune the
   decision threshold on a validation split to maximize *macro* F₀.₅ per Section 1's
   framing — expect the optimal threshold to sit meaningfully above 0.5 given the
   2:1 precision weighting.
5. **Explicitly validate singleton behavior** (Section 4.1) as its own metric
   during development: precision and recall computed *only* over the ~5.6% of
   validation entities with zero true matches. This slice is invisible in an
   overall-average view but decisive for the leaderboard.
6. **For France, do not build any new country-specific logic.** Validate
   generalization by (a) confirming your feature pipeline runs unmodified on the
   French sample rows shown in Section 6, and (b) sanity-checking that your
   *predicted* France match-count histogram resembles the country-invariant
   ground-truth shape from Section 4 (mean ≈3.46, singleton ≈5.6%) — since you have
   no labels to score against directly, distributional resemblance is your main
   signal that the model hasn't silently broken on the unseen country.

## 10. Fair-Play Boundary — What's In-Bounds vs. Out-of-Bounds

Worth stating explicitly since the France/legal-suffix discussion above sits close
to the line:

- **In-bounds:** hand-built dictionaries of generic linguistic knowledge (legal-form
  abbreviations like `SARL`→`private limited company`-equivalent, street-type
  abbreviations like `Rd`→`Road`, state/country name variants) — this is ordinary
  feature engineering using knowledge that predates and is independent of *this*
  dataset, not a lookup of *this contest's specific businesses*.
- **Out-of-bounds per the README:** calling any external company-registry API,
  geocoding service, or search engine to verify or enrich *these specific* records;
  scraping or downloading outside data to augment training. If in doubt: the test is
  "am I looking up whether this row's business is real/where it is," which is
  prohibited, vs. "am I encoding general knowledge about how the English/French/
  Hindi language abbreviates legal entities," which is not.

## 11. Research Reading List (grounded — every entry checked, with links)

Organized by what it's useful for in this specific pipeline, with a license note
where it matters for the ≤8B/MIT-Apache constraint.

**Classical & probabilistic record linkage (foundational, and the closest thing to
"designed for this exact scale")**
- Fellegi, I. & Sunter, A. (1969), *A Theory for Record Linkage* — the original
  probabilistic matching model still underlying most production record-linkage
  systems, including Splink below.
- **Splink** — [github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink):
  an Apache-2.0, open-source implementation of the Fellegi–Sunter model with EM
  parameter estimation, built specifically for linking/deduplicating **100M+
  records** on a laptop via DuckDB/Spark backends. Directly relevant given this
  dataset's scale, and license-compliant.
- **`dedupe` / `recordlinkage`** Python libraries — MIT-licensed, active-learning
  and rule-based record linkage toolkits; good for rapid blocking-rule prototyping.

**Blocking / candidate generation**
- Papadakis, G. et al. (2021), *Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity
  Resolution: A Survey*, ACM Computing Surveys — the standard survey covering
  token-, sorted-neighborhood-, and LSH-based blocking; good for justifying your
  blocking-key choices in the methodology doc.
- Paulsen, D. et al. (2023), *Sparkly: A Simple yet Surprisingly Strong TF/IDF
  Blocker for Entity Matching*, PVLDB — a strong, simple, reproducible TF-IDF
  cosine blocking baseline directly applicable to the character-n-gram blocking
  recommended in Section 8.
- Thirumuruganathan, S. et al. (2021), *Deep Learning for Blocking in Entity
  Matching: A Design Space Exploration*, PVLDB (DeepBlocker) —
  [vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p2459-thirumuruganathan.pdf](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p2459-thirumuruganathan.pdf).
  Useful if you want an embedding-based blocking channel beyond token/TF-IDF blocking.
- Zhang, W. et al. (2020), *AutoBlock: A Hands-off Automation Framework for
  Entity Matching*, WSDM — learned blocking key selection, relevant if you want to
  automate the "which token set to block on" decision rather than hand-tuning it.

**Deep / LM-based entity matching (for the residual hard-case model in Section 9)**
- Li, Y. et al. (2020), *Deep Entity Matching with Pre-trained Language Models*
  (Ditto), PVLDB — [arxiv.org/abs/2004.00584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00584).
  Casts matching as sequence-pair classification over a pretrained LM; the paper's
  own company-matching benchmark (789K × 412K records) is the closest published
  analogue to this contest's scale. Use a small/multilingual encoder to respect the
  size cap rather than their original BERT-base choice if you go this route.
- Mudgal, S. et al. (2018), *Deep Learning for Entity Matching: A Design Space
  Exploration* (DeepMatcher), SIGMOD — the original systematic comparison of neural
  architectures for EM; useful background even though GBTs are the recommended
  primary model here.
- Wu, R. et al. (2020), *ZeroER: Entity Resolution using Zero Labeled Examples*,
  SIGMOD — relevant if you want an unsupervised baseline to sanity-check your
  supervised model against, since this dataset actually gives you abundant labels.
- Wang, R. et al. (2023), *Sudowoodo: Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning for
  Multi-Purpose Data Integration and Preparation*, ICDE — contrastive pretraining
  for matching, relevant to the transliteration-bridging embedding feature in
  Section 9.

**LLM-based matching (context, not necessarily the recommended primary path given
the license/size constraint)**
- Peeters, R. & Bizer, C. (2023), *Entity Matching using Large Language Models* —
  [arxiv.org/abs/2310.11244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11244).
- Wadhwa, S. et al. (2024/2025), *Beyond Scale and Generation: Understanding
  Language-Model-based Entity Matching* — useful for understanding *why* small
  fine-tuned encoders often outperform prompted LLMs on this exact task type,
  which is the argument for the GBT+small-encoder design in Section 9 rather than
  an 8B instruction model doing pairwise judgments directly (also far too slow at
  this row count).
- Peeters, R. et al. (2023/24), *The Battleship Approach to the Low-Resource Entity
  Matching Problem* — [arxiv.org/abs/2311.15685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15685):
  relevant specifically to the France (zero-labeled-example) generalization
  challenge in Section 9.

**Multilingual / transliteration bridging (for the India script-switching and
France generalization problems)**
- Feng, F. et al. (2022), *Language-agnostic BERT Sentence Embedding* (LaBSE) —
  Apache-2.0, purpose-built for exactly this cross-script "same meaning, different
  script" similarity problem.
- Wang, L. et al. (2024), *Multilingual E5 Text Embeddings* (`multilingual-e5-
  base/-small/-large`) — **MIT-licensed**, strong multilingual retrieval-style
  embeddings, good default choice for the cross-script similarity feature in
  Section 9.
- `sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2` /
  `-mpnet-base-v2` — Apache-2.0, small (MiniLM variant ~118M params), fast enough
  to embed the full ~10M-record candidate pool.
- IBM (2025), *Granite Embedding Multilingual R2* — Apache-2.0, 200+ languages,
  a newer alternative if you want stronger retrieval quality for a similar
  parameter budget.

**Address parsing (optional, for a cleaner address-token feature)**
- `libpostal` (OpenVenues) — MIT-licensed, CRF-based multilingual address parser
  covering US, Indian, and French address conventions without any external API
  calls — fair-play-compliant since it's a static, offline, general-purpose
  parsing model, not a lookup of *this* dataset's specific businesses.

**Model-size/license shortlist that satisfies "≤8B params, MIT/Apache-2.0"** (for
the optional secondary encoder in Section 9, if you want one beyond GBTs):
`Phi-3-mini`/`Phi-3.5-mini` (MIT, 3.8B), `Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3` (Apache-2.0),
`Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B-Instruct` (Apache-2.0), plus the embedding models listed above.
**Avoid** Llama-family models (Meta community license — not MIT/Apache-2.0) and
Gemma-family models (Google's Gemma license — also not MIT/Apache-2.0) for the
final submission, even though they're popular, since they'd fail the stated
license constraint.
